The Hostel Guest-Experience Playbook

5 fixes from the front desk — the stuff that decides your reviews, your ratings, and whether guests extend their stay

By Jared Fontaine Hostel manager and work-trader across 10+ hostels in the US and Europe · Founder, Vlora Cycling Camp (Albania) · Co-author, Start Up a Successful Hostel or Airbnb

Why this guide exists

Most hostel problems are not location problems. They're systems problems — and they show up in your reviews months before you notice them yourself.

I learned that from the inside: running housekeeping with a four-person work-trader team in Hawaii, working the desk and the booking systems, and living the work-trade life in hostels across Italy, France, Germany, Switzerland, Ireland, Scotland, Puerto Rico, and Croatia. At one hostel, a simple booking system I built cut overbookings by 30%. At another, a disciplined deep-clean rota moved our cleanliness rating on TripAdvisor — the score guests actually filter by.

This playbook is the five fixes I'd make first at any hostel. Every one of them is cheap. Most are free. All of them compound into better reviews.

Fix 1 — Kill bed bugs for about $50, before they kill your reviews

If you host hundreds of guests, bed bugs are not a possibility, they're a schedule. The only question is whether you prevent them or pay for them.

Professional removal costs thousands and usually comes after the review that says "bed bugs" — the two words that can halve a hostel's bookings overnight. Prevention costs about $50:

  1. Diatomaceous earth on every mattress, under the linens, at every bed change. It kills on contact, naturally, and keeps working for up to six months.
  2. Eco-friendly contact spray on mattresses every few weeks between guests. The pine-scented ones make the room smell fresher, not chemical.
  3. Make it a rota item, not a reaction. Prevention belongs on the cleaning checklist, owned by a named person, every single turnover.

Since I started running this protocol, I've dealt with thousands of guests without another infestation. That's the whole trick: boring, routine, done before there's a problem.

Check yourself: Could a new work-trader walk in tomorrow and find this protocol written down? If it lives in your head, it doesn't exist.

Fix 2 — End overbookings with one live roster

Overbookings don't come from bad software. They come from three booking channels, a paper notebook, and a spreadsheet that one person updates "later."

The fix that cut overbookings by 30% at the hostel I worked in Hawaii was embarrassingly simple:

  1. One roster, bed-level. Every bed has a line. Every line has a guest name and a checkout date. Nothing exists unless it's on the roster.
  2. Every channel lands in the same place. Booking.com, Hostelworld, Airbnb, walk-ins — entered at the moment of booking or check-in, by whoever took it, on their phone.
  3. Morning checkout check. First job of the day: read the roster, mark today's departures, know your real availability before the platforms sell a bed you don't have.

You don't need expensive property software to do this. You need one source of truth and the discipline to keep it live.

Check yourself: If I asked for tonight's true availability right now, how many places would your staff have to look?

Fix 3 — Treat cleanliness as a rating you manage, not a chore you do

Guests forgive old buildings. They don't forgive dirty bathrooms — and cleanliness is a scored category on the platforms that send you bookings.

What worked for us:

  1. A weekly deep-clean schedule, written down, rotating through the whole property — not just the visible surfaces. Kitchens, drains, mattress checks, the corners guests photograph.
  2. A named team with a rota. I managed four work-traders on scheduled shifts. Cleaning fails when it's everyone's job in general and nobody's job today.
  3. Inspect like a guest. Walk your own dorm with a phone camera once a week. Whatever you'd photograph, a guest already has.

Check yourself: When did someone last deep-clean the room your worst review mentions?

Fix 4 — Work-traders: recruit for attitude, keep them with clarity

Work-traders are the backbone of most hostels — and the #1 source of chaos when they're managed badly. They work for a bed, so money can't motivate them. Clarity and experience can.

  1. Recruit in person where possible. A few days as a guest tells you more than any application form. The hostels that struggle hire strangers off messages; the ones that run well hire people they've watched be a decent guest.
  2. Put the deal in writing on day one. Hours, days off, what the bed includes, how long the stay is. Most work-trader blow-ups are just two different memories of a verbal deal.
  3. Give them the good stuff too. Tours, local knowledge, a real welcome into the place. A work-trader who feels like staff stays longer, works better, and tells the next good one to come. A work-trader who feels like free labour leaves mid-season — usually at your busiest week.

Check yourself: If your best work-trader quit tomorrow, is there a written system the next one could pick up — or does the knowledge leave with them?

Fix 5 — Make the hostel the experience, not just the bed

The best hostels I've stayed in or worked at — including ones in terrible locations — shared the same pattern: guests arrived planning one night and stayed a week. That's pure margin, and it comes from deliberate touches:

  1. The welcome is a ritual, not a transaction. Walk the guest to their room. Give them a map, mark where they are, where to eat, and your favourite place — the personal recommendation is what they remember.
  2. One big table. A communal breakfast — even a simple one, packed early for guests leaving at dawn — creates the social engine no hotel can copy. Hostels sell belonging; the table is where it happens.
  3. Program the week. Free walking tour, a hike, a game night, one free beer to get the room talking. Activities turn strangers into a group, and groups extend stays and write the reviews that mention your hostel by name.

Check yourself: What happens at your hostel at 8pm tonight that a guest would put in a review?

Your 60-second scorecard

Give yourself 1 point for each honest "yes":

  • [ ] Bed-bug prevention is written down and on the cleaning rota
  • [ ] One live, bed-level roster is the only source of truth for bookings
  • [ ] Every channel (platforms + walk-ins) is entered the moment it happens
  • [ ] A weekly deep-clean schedule exists, with named owners
  • [ ] Work-trader terms are written down on day one
  • [ ] You could lose your best staff member and the systems would survive
  • [ ] Check-in includes a map, a personal recommendation, and a walk to the room
  • [ ] Something social happens at your hostel every evening
  • [ ] You reply to every review — good and bad — within 48 hours
  • [ ] Your last 10 reviews mention staff, cleanliness, or atmosphere (not just location)

8–10: You're running a system. An audit would be about fine-tuning. 5–7: The gaps above are costing you rating points you can win back in a month. 0–4: Your reviews are being written by your systems, not your intentions — this is fixable, and it's the cheapest revenue you'll ever recover.
